Commercial strategy case on Brazil’s largest marketplace aggregator — from raw transaction logs to executive decisions

From marketplace data to commercial strategy

Rapid top-line expansion often hides fragile unit economics. Working through 99,441 orders from Olist’s Brazilian marketplace, I built the analytical infrastructure to answer four executive questions — and then designed a decision path for each. Every finding moves through the same discipline: identify the commercial problem, isolate the operational pain point, design a solution that improves unit economics, and lock in the operations — so leadership can act, defer, or stop on committed evidence.

Modern ELT Pipeline Design — every stage is a commercial safeguard, not a technical ritual. Data architecture is only as valuable as the executive decisions it governs.

The mandateDiagnose why a 7.5× growth marketplace stalled in 2018 — and design a plan to fix its 96.9% buyer churn, its 24% peak state-level late rate, and its 67.6% seller concentration.

The evidence base99,441 orders across 96,096 unique buyers and 3,095 sellers (Sept 2016 – Oct 2018). All monetary figures in Brazilian reais (R$).

What leadership getsA prioritised commercial roadmap, with every recommendation tied to a KPI the board can watch — and a stop rule. Built on SQL Server (star schema for single-source KPI governance) and Python (audits and stress tests), but designed for the decision, not the query.

Step 1: Start from the executive mandate

In
9 disconnected operational exports
Out
Four commercial pillars for leadership

Olist operates as a B2B2C marketplace aggregator: it enables thousands of small Brazilian merchants to sell through unified storefronts and ship via third-party carriers. Raw transaction logs record 99,441 orders across nine tables, but leadership cannot steer a P&L from raw logs when order grains, customer identities and logistics timestamps are fragmented.

So before writing a single analytical query, I organised the work around four questions that determine marketplace survival — the four lenses a CEO, CFO, COO and CCO would each put on this business:

  1. 1Growth architecture: Is top-line GMV compounding organically, or masking an acquisition-driven plateau?
  2. 2Fulfilment & unit economics: Where do broken delivery promises and freight ratios destroy margin?
  3. 3Retention & LTV: Why do 96.9% of buyers never return, and what operational threshold triggers churn?
  4. 4Supply risk & basket size: How exposed is GMV to top-seller churn, and why are baskets stuck at one item?

The order matters: growth and fulfilment are P&L questions the board can read this quarter; retention and supply are risk questions that decide whether the business survives the next two years.

Noise upstream is strategy risk downstream. Unchecked anomalies lead to flawed capital allocation — a double-counted state inflates a regional SLA story, an unmapped category hides a P&L problem, and duplicate reviews move NPS enough to justify or kill a retention programme. So before modelling the warehouse, I resolved three upstream distortions that would have corrupted regional SLAs, category P&Ls and satisfaction scores:

Regional GMV fan-out1,000,163 location rows with multiple coordinates per zip codeCollapsed to 19,015 unique zip centroids with modal city/state attribution
Unattributed revenue73 Portuguese category names and 610 unmapped productsStandardised to 71 English categories, 2 retained Portuguese niches, and an explicit “Unknown” bucket

WITH Geo_Frequency AS (
    SELECT
        geolocation_zip_code_prefix,
        geolocation_city,
        geolocation_state,
        COUNT(*) as freq,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix ORDER BY COUNT(*) DESC) as rn
    FROM olist.geolocation
    GROUP BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix, geolocation_city, geolocation_state
),
Geo_Centroids AS (
    SELECT
        geolocation_zip_code_prefix,
        AVG(geolocation_lat) as avg_lat,
        AVG(geolocation_lng) as avg_lng
    FROM olist.geolocation
    GROUP BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix
)
SELECT
    c.geolocation_zip_code_prefix AS zip_code,
    c.avg_lat,
    c.avg_lng,
    f.geolocation_city AS city,
    f.geolocation_state AS state
INTO dwh.Dim_Geolocation
FROM Geo_Centroids c
JOIN Geo_Frequency f ON c.geolocation_zip_code_prefix = f.geolocation_zip_code_prefix
WHERE f.rn = 1;

Step 4: Architect the Star Schema around commercial grain

In
Cleaned staging tables
Out
2 fact tables & 5 dimensions in schema dwh
SQL
02_transform_build_dwh.sql

Grain is a commercial choice, not a technical one. Mixing multi-item line rows with order-level delivery metrics is the fastest way to double-count revenue and distort late-delivery rates — the two numbers the whole logistics case rests on. So I separated Fact_Orders (99,441 rows, pre-aggregating item prices and freight to the exact order grain) from Fact_Order_Items (112,650 rows, preserving item-level granularity for seller share, freight ratios and cross-sell attach).

Identifying the real buyer for LTV. Olist assigns a new customer_id to every checkout, meaning 99,441 transaction IDs actually belong to 96,096 unique individuals (customer_unique_id). Modelling Dim_Customer around the underlying person — rather than the checkout token — is what exposes the platform’s true repeat-purchase economics. Get this wrong and the churn story disappears.

Read the SQL: pre-aggregating financials to protect Fact_Orders grain 
-- FIX: Calculate Financials first (Pre-Aggregation)
-- We must aggregate items to the Order grain to join them to Fact_Orders
WITH Order_Financials AS (
    SELECT
        order_id,
        SUM(price) AS total_order_value,
        SUM(freight_value) AS total_freight,
        COUNT(*) AS items_count
    FROM olist.order_items
    GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT
    o.order_id,
    o.customer_id,
    o.order_status,
    o.order_purchase_timestamp,

    -- Financials (Added)
    ISNULL(f.total_order_value, 0) AS total_order_value,
    ISNULL(f.total_freight, 0) AS total_freight,
    ISNULL(f.items_count, 0) AS items_count,

    -- Logistics
    CASE
        WHEN o.order_delivered_customer_date < o.order_purchase_timestamp THEN NULL
        ELSE DATEDIFF(DAY, o.order_purchase_timestamp, o.order_delivered_customer_date)
    END AS actual_lead_time_days,

    DATEDIFF(DAY, o.order_delivered_customer_date, o.order_estimated_delivery_date) AS delivery_gap_days,

    -- Flags
    CASE WHEN o.order_status = 'delivered' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_delivered,
    CASE WHEN o.order_status IN ('canceled', 'unavailable') THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_canceled,

    CASE
        WHEN o.order_delivered_customer_date > o.order_estimated_delivery_date THEN 1
        ELSE 0
    END AS is_late_delivery,

    -- Dimensions
    c.customer_city,
    c.customer_state,
    c.customer_zip_code_prefix AS customer_zip

INTO dwh.Fact_Orders
FROM olist.orders o
LEFT JOIN olist.customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
LEFT JOIN Order_Financials f ON o.order_id = f.order_id;

03_business_queries.sql, part A

Different teams, different numbers, is the fastest way to lose a board. When Finance, Operations and Marketing each write their own joins, executive meetings turn into debates over whose numbers are right rather than which decision to take. I built vw_order_metrics as a governed semantic layer at the one-row-per-order grain (purchase_ts, gmv, delivery_gap_days) to enforce two company-wide business rules in code:

  • Primary category attribution: Multi-item orders are attributed to the category of their highest-value item (ORDER BY price DESC), preventing double-counted order volume across category managers — and giving each category owner a clean, honest P&L.
  • Standardised NPS buckets: Review scores of 1–2 stars classify as Detractor, 4–5 stars as Promoter, and 3 stars as Passive, directly tying logistics SLA breaches to customer sentiment so the retention programme has a live read-out.

1. Growth architecture: 7.5× expansion, then an acquisition plateau

Queries Q1 and Q3

1. Identify the problem

On the surface, delivered GMV grew 7.5× from R$112K in January 2017 to R$839K in August 2018. Decomposing the monthly trajectory, however, reveals a structural stall: after spiking to R$991K during Black Friday (November 2017), revenue flattened between R$0.83M and R$0.98M a month throughout 2018 — bringing Jan–Aug 2018 growth down to 2.4× YoY.

2. Find the pain point

Olist is running on a feast-or-famine acquisition treadmill. Revenue is anchored by three hero verticals — Health & Beauty (R$1.23M), Watches & Gifts (R$1.17M) and Bed, Bath & Table (R$1.03M) — yet because 96.9% of buyers never place a second order, past cohorts contribute almost no compounding baseline to 2018 GMV. When paid acquisition or seasonal traffic slows, the top line flattens immediately.

3. Show the solution

Shift capital allocation from broad seasonal acquisition spikes to category-led lifecycle compounding: use natural replenishment cycles in Health & Beauty (the #1 revenue vertical) as the primary repeat-purchase engine, and use Bed, Bath & Table as the anchor for multi-item home bundles — turning one-off holiday traffic into recurring quarterly demand.

4. The operations

Re-anchor executive reporting in vw_order_metrics to split monthly GMV into first-order GMV vs. repeat-cohort GMV by primary category. Ring-fence CRM replenishment triggers at Day 30 and Day 60 specifically for delivered Health & Beauty buyers, and require pre-committed holdout lift targets before approving Q4 promotional subsidies.

Read the SQL: Q3 monthly revenue trajectory 
-- Q3. Monthly Sales Trend (Seasonality)
SELECT
    FORMAT(purchase_date, 'yyyy-MM') AS Sales_Month,
    FORMAT(SUM(gmv), 'C', 'en-US') AS Revenue
FROM dwh.vw_order_metrics
WHERE order_status = 'delivered' AND purchase_date >= '2017-01-01'
GROUP BY FORMAT(purchase_date, 'yyyy-MM')
ORDER BY Sales_Month DESC;

2. Fulfilment & unit economics: carrier transit bottlenecks and the freight trap

Queries Q2, Q7, Q9, Q10 and Q11

1. Identify the problem

Across Brazil, 8.1% of delivered orders miss the promised delivery date, while 2,963 orders (3.0%) never reach “delivered” at all — direct GMV and trust leakage. At the same time, shipping costs relative to item prices are choking conversion in low-ticket categories.

2. Find the pain point

Decomposing order timestamps across the three fulfilment stages proves that sellers are not the bottleneck: payment approval takes under 1 day and merchants hand parcels to carriers within 2.5–3.3 days. The failure sits entirely in carrier line-haul transit (20–26 days in the slowest states), pushing late rates to 24.0% in Alagoas and 15.2%–19.6% across Maranhão, Piauí, Ceará and Sergipe — while São Paulo (2,391 late orders) and Rio de Janeiro (1,671) generate the largest absolute volume. Worse, 1,101 orders have been stuck at “shipped” for 60+ days, and in Electronics (median item price R$21.90), freight averages 68.8% of the product price — costing more than the item itself on 22% of orders.

3. Show the solution

Execute a three-part logistics & freight re-architecture: (1) decouple checkout ETA algorithms from uniform national averages by adding dynamic regional carrier buffers in the Northeast and Rio de Janeiro; (2) shift carrier contracts to performance-tiered SLAs rather than penalising merchants who already pack in under 3.3 days; and (3) introduce a minimum cart threshold for flat-rate shipping on small Electronics so buyers bundle accessories rather than abandoning R$21.90 items over freight shock.

4. The operations

Implement an automated 30-day in-transit exception trigger in SQL to flag lost shipments, initiate carrier claims and auto-refund customers — catching the 1,101 stranded orders before they become chargebacks. Track Seller Pack SLA (under 3 days) separately from Carrier Transit SLA by state route, and reject premature warehouse CAPEX in the North (Roraima, Amapá and Amazonas totalled just 254 orders over two years — far below break-even density).

Read the SQL: Q11 stage-by-stage logistics bottleneck decomposition 
-- Q11. Logistics Breakdown (Bottleneck Analysis)
-- Insight: Does the delay happen at Approval, Packing (Seller), or Transit (Carrier)?
SELECT TOP 5
    customer_state,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, purchase_ts, approved_ts))/24.0 as Days_To_Approve,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, approved_ts, carrier_ts))/24.0 as Days_To_Pack,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, carrier_ts, delivered_ts))/24.0 as Days_In_Transit
FROM dwh.vw_order_metrics
WHERE order_status = 'delivered'
GROUP BY customer_state
ORDER BY Days_In_Transit DESC;

3. Customer retention & channel fit: the 96.9% churn wall and the 20-day NPS cliff

Queries Q4, Q5, Q6 and Q14

1. Identify the problem

Olist is functioning as a one-time customer rental channel, not a compounding marketplace: out of 96,096 unique buyers, 93,099 (96.9%) purchased only once, leaving just 2,997 repeat customers across two years. When LTV is capped at a single order, customer acquisition cost cannot be amortised — the growth in the previous finding is being bought, not compounded.

2. Find the pain point

Joining Fact_Orders to Dim_Reviews pinpoints the exact operational threshold where trust breaks: the 20-day transit cliff. Detractor rates (1–2 stars) stay contained at 7.2% for 0–5 day deliveries (4.44★), 8.4% for 6–10 days (4.35★) and 10.5% for 11–20 days (4.21★), then spike 3.5× to 37.0% (3.16★) once delivery exceeds 20 days. Meanwhile, demand is heavily skewed to weekday work hours — peaking Monday–Tuesday at ~175 orders/day between 10:00 and 16:00 vs. 123 on Saturdays — and 19.9% of volume relies on boleto bank slips (vs. 76.9% credit cards), adding multi-day clearing delays before a parcel even ships.

3. Show the solution

Replace blanket win-back discounting with experience-gated CRM & workday channel alignment: (1) gate second-order incentives strictly to buyers whose first order arrived within 10 days (4.35–4.44★ promoters), avoiding subsidy waste on detractors burned by 20+ day transit; (2) reallocate paid marketing from weekend consumer slots into Monday–Friday daytime windows (10:00–16:00) while validating how much of that demand is B2B/office procurement; and (3) offer instant-checkout incentives to migrate boleto users onto faster-clearing payment rails.

4. The operations

Automate cohort segmentation inside vw_order_metrics using actual_lead_time_days <= 10 and nps_bucket = 'Promoter'. Run a controlled 90-day A/B holdout test on post-delivery repeat vouchers, add a lightweight CNPJ (business tax ID) toggle at checkout to size B2B demand before building dedicated features, and cap boleto inventory holds at 48 hours.

Read the SQL: Q5 true customer retention across unique individuals 
-- Q5. Customer Retention Rate (Lifetime Value Check)
WITH CustomerFrequency AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) as Lifetime_Orders
    FROM dwh.Dim_Customer c
    JOIN dwh.Fact_Orders f ON c.customer_id = f.customer_id
    GROUP BY customer_unique_id
)
SELECT
    CASE WHEN Lifetime_Orders = 1 THEN 'One-Time Buyer' ELSE 'Repeat Buyer' END AS Buyer_Type,
    COUNT(*) as Customer_Count,
    FORMAT(COUNT(*) * 1.0 / (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM CustomerFrequency), 'P') as Share_of_Base
FROM CustomerFrequency
GROUP BY CASE WHEN Lifetime_Orders = 1 THEN 'One-Time Buyer' ELSE 'Repeat Buyer' END;

4. Supply concentration & basket economics: protecting the top 10% and unlocking attach

Queries Q8, Q12 and Q13

1. Identify the problem

Olist faces severe supply-side concentration alongside under-monetised buyer baskets: the top 10% of sellers (310 of 3,095) control 67.6% of total item revenue — the top 100 alone generate 45.1% — while the bottom 50% contribute just 3.2%. Simultaneously, 96.7% of orders contain only a single distinct product.

2. Find the pain point

This Pareto skew creates two commercial vulnerabilities. First, if competing Brazilian marketplaces poach even 20–30 of Olist’s top 100 sellers, platform GMV drops immediately — the concentration is the risk. Second, cross-referencing seller volume against review scores exposes a pocket of brand-damaging volume: one top-20 seller averages 3.35★ across 982 orders, and five mid-to-large sellers (50+ orders) average under 3.0★ while contributing only 0.6% of platform revenue. On the demand side, product discovery fails to cross-sell: only 3.3% of orders include two distinct products and 0.8% span two categories (led by Bed, Bath & Table + Furniture & Décor at 70 orders).

3. Show the solution

Launch tiered seller portfolio governance & cross-category merchandising: (1) lock in the top 100–310 anchor sellers with dedicated key-account management, faster payout terms and volume-tiered commission rebates, while incubating Decile 2–3 merchants (22.5% of GMV) to diversify concentration risk; (2) put the five sub-3.0★ high-volume sellers on a 30-day quality remediation gate — risking just 0.6% of GMV to eliminate thousands of detractor experiences; and (3) deploy automated “Frequently Bought Together” bundles pairing Bed, Bath & Table with Furniture & Décor and Housewares at checkout.

4. The operations

Run automated monthly NTILE(10) seller scorecards in the warehouse tracking GMV share, cancellation rate, pack SLA and rolling 90-day review average. Set a hard governance rule: any seller exceeding 50 orders with a rating below 3.5★ is automatically throttled in search impressions until SLA compliance is restored. Measure cross-sell module performance via multi-SKU attach rate (targeting a lift from 3.3% toward 6%+) and incremental AOV against a checkout holdout group.

Read the SQL: Q8 Pareto decile concentration across 3,095 sellers 
-- Q8. Seller Concentration (Pareto Principle)
WITH SellerSales AS (
    SELECT seller_id, SUM(price) as Revenue
    FROM dwh.Fact_Order_Items
    GROUP BY seller_id
),
RankedSellers AS (
    SELECT Revenue, NTILE(10) OVER (ORDER BY Revenue DESC) as Decile
    FROM SellerSales
)
SELECT
    Decile,
    FORMAT(SUM(Revenue), 'C', 'en-US') as Total_Revenue,
    FORMAT(SUM(Revenue) * 1.0 / (SELECT SUM(Revenue) FROM SellerSales), 'P') as Share_of_Total
FROM RankedSellers
GROUP BY Decile
ORDER BY Decile;

Step 7: Executive roadmap & capital allocation

In
Four structural diagnoses
Out
Phased commercial strategy, operating owners & CAPEX guardrails

Sequencing logic. A strategy is a choice about order, not a list of good ideas. Horizon 1 fixes leakage that costs revenue today without new capital. Horizon 2 builds compounding assets — retention, seller incubation, cross-sell — that only pay off once the leaks are sealed. Running them the other way round would pour cash into a broken funnel.

Horizon 1 (Months 1–3): stop leakage & protect core GMV

  1. 1
    Recalibrate Northeast & RJ carrier SLAs and checkout ETAs

    Stop penalising merchants (who pack in under 3.3 days) for 20–26 day carrier transit delays. Inject route-specific transit buffers into checkout ETAs in Alagoas (24.0% late), Maranhão (19.6%) and Rio de Janeiro (1,671 late orders), and renegotiate 3PL line-haul penalties to keep deliveries under the 20-day NPS cliff.

    KPI gate: state late-delivery rate <10% & 20+ day detractor share

  2. 2
    Automate 30-day carrier exception sweeps for stranded orders

    Resolve the 1,101 paid orders stuck at “shipped” for 60+ days. Deploy an automated SQL exception trigger at Day 30 in transit to file carrier claims and proactively refund or reship — eliminating silent brand erosion.

    KPI gate: zero orders stuck in “shipped” beyond 30 days

  3. 3
    Gate sub-3.0★ high-volume sellers on a 30-day SLA remediation plan

    Isolate the five sellers with 50+ orders averaging under 3.0★ (just 0.6% of total GMV) plus the 3.35★ top-20 outlier (982 orders). Enforce mandatory quality coaching or search demotion to protect marketplace trust at minimal revenue risk.

    KPI gate: defect & cancellation rate across audited sellers

  4. 4
    Launch experience-gated second-order lifecycle triggers

    Break the 96.9% one-time buyer wall without burning margin on blanket discounts. Trigger 30-day replenishment incentives in Health & Beauty exclusively to first-time buyers whose order arrived in 10 days or fewer (4.35–4.44★ promoters).

    KPI gate: 90-day repeat purchase lift vs. 50/50 holdout control

Horizon 2 (Months 4–12): compound LTV, supply leverage & baskets

  1. 5
    Lock in top 100–310 sellers and incubate Decile 2–3 supply

    Protect the 67.6% of GMV held by the top 10% of sellers with dedicated account management, faster working-capital payouts and volume-tiered commission rebates, while giving Decile 2–3 sellers (22.5% of GMV) co-funded traffic tools to reduce concentration risk.

    KPI gate: top-100 seller annualised retention & Decile 2–3 GMV share

  2. 6
    Deploy cross-category bundling in Home & Living verticals

    Move multi-product order share above 3.3% by embedding “Frequently Bought Together” prompts pairing Bed, Bath & Table with Furniture & Décor and Housewares on product and cart pages.

    KPI gate: multi-SKU attach rate & incremental AOV vs. control

  3. 7
    Introduce basket-threshold shipping for low-ticket Electronics

    Fix the 68.8% freight-to-price trap on R$21.90 median Electronics orders. Replace single-item flat freight with a minimum cart threshold that unlocks subsidised shipping when buyers add accessories or a second item.

    KPI gate: Electronics checkout conversion & net freight margin per order

  4. 8
    Align acquisition to workday peaks & validate B2B checkout demand

    Shift paid media toward Monday–Friday 10:00–16:00 peak conversion windows, and add a zero-cost CNPJ (business tax ID) field at checkout to quantify B2B procurement share before investing in dedicated corporate invoicing features.

    KPI gate: weekday ROAS lift & verified B2B share of checkout GMV

Deliberately deferred. A decision to act is also a decision about what not to act on. Three tempting paths were rejected: (1) A Northern fulfilment hub — Roraima, Amapá and Amazonas together produced just 254 delivered orders in two years, too small to amortise warehouse CAPEX; fix checkout ETAs and 3PL contracts instead. (2) A blanket price war against Shopee and Amazon — erodes margin without addressing the 96.9% churn wall, and Google/Instagram CPCs suggest this costs more than it recovers. (3) A dedicated B2B storefront — the weekday-concentrated order pattern is suggestive but not proof; validate with a zero-cost CNPJ toggle before committing engineering capacity.

How I take a marketplace from raw data to an executive decision

  • 1. Diagnose the commercial problem, not the symptom

    Look past the headline number (7.5× GMV growth) to the structural constraint underneath it — a 2018 plateau, 96.9% churn, 67.6% supply concentration — because the headline is what the board already knows, and the constraint is what they need to see.

  • 2. Isolate the pain point and the true operational owner

    Decompose end-to-end workflows to find where the metric actually breaks (carrier transit, not seller pack time), so the recommendation names a specific team and a specific lever — not an industry.

  • 3. Design a solution that improves unit economics, not just GMV

    Gate retention spend to happy cohorts, solve freight traps with basket thresholds, and size demand before committing CAPEX — every recommendation is tested against the P&L, not just against volume.

  • 4. Build the operations that make the plan runnable

    Ship each recommendation with a committed decision gate, a KPI the board can watch and a holdout comparison — so leadership can act, iterate or stop on evidence, not on faith.
