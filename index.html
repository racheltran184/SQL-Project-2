End-to-end SQL Server project on the Brazilian E-Commerce Public Dataset by Olist

Nine raw files, one warehouse, fourteen questions

I loaded a Brazilian marketplace’s public data into SQL Server, modelled it as a star schema and asked it fourteen business questions. The answers point the same way: almost every customer buys once, slow deliveries drag the reviews down, and 310 sellers carry two-thirds of the revenue.

  1. Step 2Raw files9 CSVs, 1,550,922 rows
  2. Step 3Clean1,000,163 location rows become 19,015 zip codes
  3. Step 4Star schema2 fact tables, 5 dimensions
  4. Step 5One viewvw_order_metrics, one row per order

Each step below lists what went in, what came out and the SQL behind it.

My roleBuilt end to end: loading, cleaning, warehouse design, the SQL analysis and the charts.

The data99,441 orders placed between September 2016 and October 2018. Money is in Brazilian reais (R$).

The toolsSQL Server and T-SQL (CTEs, window functions, views); Python with pandas and seaborn for the charts and the re-check.

Step 1: Start from the business questions

In
A marketplace’s public data export
Out
Four questions for the warehouse

Olist lets small Brazilian merchants sell through one online store and ship with its logistics partners. Its public export covers 99,441 orders split across nine files: orders, items, payments, reviews, customers, sellers, products, locations and a category translation table.

The analysis is organised around four questions:

  1. 1Is growth steady, or driven by one-off peaks?
  2. 2Where are deliveries late, and which leg of the journey is slow?
  3. 3Do customers come back, and what moves their reviews?
  4. 4How much rests on a few sellers, and do customers buy more than one thing?

Three problems would have multiplied rows or left gaps in the results, so each is fixed while its dimension table is built.

ProblemBeforeAfter
Locations1,000,163 rows, many per zip code19,015 zip codes, each with an average latitude and longitude and its most common city and state
Categories73 Portuguese names; 610 products with none71 English names, 2 kept in Portuguese, and “Unknown” for the blanks
Reviews99,224 rows; 814 review IDs repeated98,410 reviews, keeping the latest answer for each

Lead times are guarded too: a delivery date earlier than the purchase date becomes NULL rather than a negative number of days.

Read the SQL: zip codes and categories 
WITH Geo_Frequency AS (
    SELECT
        geolocation_zip_code_prefix,
        geolocation_city,
        geolocation_state,
        COUNT(*) as freq,
        ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix ORDER BY COUNT(*) DESC) as rn
    FROM olist.geolocation
    GROUP BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix, geolocation_city, geolocation_state
),
Geo_Centroids AS (
    SELECT
        geolocation_zip_code_prefix,
        AVG(geolocation_lat) as avg_lat,
        AVG(geolocation_lng) as avg_lng
    FROM olist.geolocation
    GROUP BY geolocation_zip_code_prefix
)
SELECT
    c.geolocation_zip_code_prefix AS zip_code,
    c.avg_lat,
    c.avg_lng,
    f.geolocation_city AS city,
    f.geolocation_state AS state
INTO dwh.Dim_Geolocation
FROM Geo_Centroids c
JOIN Geo_Frequency f ON c.geolocation_zip_code_prefix = f.geolocation_zip_code_prefix
WHERE f.rn = 1;

Step 4: Shape it as a star schema

In
The cleaned tables
Out
2 fact tables and 5 dimensions in schema dwh, each with a primary key
SQL
02_transform_build_dwh.sql

Grain first. Fact_Orders holds one row per order. Item prices and freight are summed to order level before the join, so an order with three items still counts once. Fact_Order_Items keeps one row per item for the product, seller and basket questions.

The real customer. Every order gets its own customer_id, so 99,441 ids belong to 96,096 people. Dim_Customer keeps customer_unique_id, the person, which is what the repeat-buyer question has to count.

Read the SQL: Fact_Orders 
-- FIX: Calculate Financials first (Pre-Aggregation)
-- We must aggregate items to the Order grain to join them to Fact_Orders
WITH Order_Financials AS (
    SELECT
        order_id,
        SUM(price) AS total_order_value,
        SUM(freight_value) AS total_freight,
        COUNT(*) AS items_count
    FROM olist.order_items
    GROUP BY order_id
)
SELECT
    o.order_id,
    o.customer_id,
    o.order_status,
    o.order_purchase_timestamp,

    -- Financials (Added)
    ISNULL(f.total_order_value, 0) AS total_order_value,
    ISNULL(f.total_freight, 0) AS total_freight,
    ISNULL(f.items_count, 0) AS items_count,

    -- Logistics
    CASE
        WHEN o.order_delivered_customer_date < o.order_purchase_timestamp THEN NULL
        ELSE DATEDIFF(DAY, o.order_purchase_timestamp, o.order_delivered_customer_date)
    END AS actual_lead_time_days,

    DATEDIFF(DAY, o.order_delivered_customer_date, o.order_estimated_delivery_date) AS delivery_gap_days,

    -- Flags
    CASE WHEN o.order_status = 'delivered' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_delivered,
    CASE WHEN o.order_status IN ('canceled', 'unavailable') THEN 1 ELSE 0 END AS is_canceled,

    CASE
        WHEN o.order_delivered_customer_date > o.order_estimated_delivery_date THEN 1
        ELSE 0
    END AS is_late_delivery,

    -- Dimensions
    c.customer_city,
    c.customer_state,
    c.customer_zip_code_prefix AS customer_zip

INTO dwh.Fact_Orders
FROM olist.orders o
LEFT JOIN olist.customers c ON o.customer_id = c.customer_id
LEFT JOIN Order_Financials f ON o.order_id = f.order_id;

Step 5: Put one view in front of the analysts

In
The seven warehouse tables
Out
vw_order_metrics, one row per order
SQL
03_business_queries.sql, part A

Fourteen questions shouldn’t mean fourteen copies of the same joins. vw_order_metrics joins Fact_Orders to customers and reviews, renames columns for reading (purchase_ts, gmv, and delivery_gap_days, where a positive number means early) and fixes two business rules in one place:

  • An order’s category is the category of its most expensive item.
  • A 1 or 2 star review counts as a detractor, 4 or 5 as a promoter, and 3 as passive.

Eight of the fourteen queries read from this view; the other six go straight to the fact tables.

Read the SQL: the two business rules 
    -- Product Category Logic:

    CASE
        WHEN r.review_score <= 2 THEN 'Detractor'
        WHEN r.review_score >= 4 THEN 'Promoter'
        ELSE 'Passive'
    END AS nps_bucket

Growth: 7.5× in twenty months, then a plateau

Queries Q1 and Q3

What the numbers said

Delivered revenue rose from R$112K in January 2017 to R$839K in August 2018, 7.5×. It peaked in November 2017, the month of Black Friday, at R$991K. Through 2018 it stayed between R$0.83M and R$0.98M a month: January to August 2018 was 2.4× the same months of 2017, not 7.5×.

Health & beauty (R$1.23M), watches & gifts (R$1.17M) and bed, bath & table (R$1.03M) lead the ten biggest categories.

What it means

The first year was growth from a small base, and by 2018 the curve is flat. More revenue now has to come from existing customers ordering again, which the customer finding shows almost never happens.

Read the SQL: Q3 monthly trend 
-- Q3. Monthly Sales Trend (Seasonality)
SELECT
    FORMAT(purchase_date, 'yyyy-MM') AS Sales_Month,
    FORMAT(SUM(gmv), 'C', 'en-US') AS Revenue
FROM dwh.vw_order_metrics
WHERE order_status = 'delivered' AND purchase_date >= '2017-01-01'
GROUP BY FORMAT(purchase_date, 'yyyy-MM')
ORDER BY Sales_Month DESC;

Deliveries: the carrier leg is where the days go

Queries Q2, Q7, Q9, Q10 and Q11

What the numbers said

8.1% of delivered orders arrived after the date the customer was given. Late rates are highest in the Northeast: Alagoas 24.0%, Maranhão 19.6%, Piauí 15.9%, Ceará 15.4% and Sergipe 15.2%. By count, São Paulo and Rio de Janeiro still produce the most late orders, 2,391 and 1,671.

In the five slowest states, payment approval takes under a day and sellers hand parcels to the carrier within 2.5 to 3.3 days, while transit averages 20 to 26 days. These states are small, though: Roraima, the slowest, had 41 delivered orders.

2,963 orders (3.0%) never reached “delivered”. Of the 1,107 stuck at “shipped”, 1,101 were bought at least 60 days before the data ends.

Shipping weighs most on electronics: 68.8% of the item price on average. The typical electronics order is cheap (median R$21.90), and on 22% of them shipping costs more than the item.

What it means

Delivery promises are least reliable in the Northeast, and the slow leg is transit, so the fix sits with carriers and with the dates shown at checkout rather than with sellers. Small, cheap items are where freight hurts most.

Read the SQL: Q11 where the days go 
-- Q11. Logistics Breakdown (Bottleneck Analysis)
-- Insight: Does the delay happen at Approval, Packing (Seller), or Transit (Carrier)?
SELECT TOP 5
    customer_state,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, purchase_ts, approved_ts))/24.0 as Days_To_Approve,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, approved_ts, carrier_ts))/24.0 as Days_To_Pack,
    AVG(DATEDIFF(HOUR, carrier_ts, delivered_ts))/24.0 as Days_In_Transit
FROM dwh.vw_order_metrics
WHERE order_status = 'delivered'
GROUP BY customer_state
ORDER BY Days_In_Transit DESC;

Customers: 97% order once, and slow deliveries cost stars

Queries Q4, Q5, Q6 and Q14

What the numbers said

Of 96,096 customers, 93,099 (96.9%) ordered only once. Just 2,997 came back.

Reviews hold up until delivery passes 20 days, then fall sharply: 37.0% of reviews for orders that took more than 20 days are 1 or 2 stars, against 7.2% for orders delivered within 5 days. The average drops from 4.44 to 3.16 stars.

Orders peak on weekdays, about 175 a day on Mondays and Tuesdays against 123 on Saturdays, with busy hours from 10:00 to 16:00 and again from 20:00 to 22:00. A third of orders come in after 18:00. Credit cards pay for 76.9% of orders, and boleto, a bank payment slip, for 19.9%.

What it means

Retention is the biggest gap, and delivery time is the lever the data can see. The weekday pattern could mean people shop from work, or that some buyers are businesses; the data can’t tell these apart, so it’s a question to test rather than a conclusion.

Read the SQL: Q5 repeat buyers 
-- Q5. Customer Retention Rate (Lifetime Value Check)
WITH CustomerFrequency AS (
    SELECT customer_unique_id, COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) as Lifetime_Orders
    FROM dwh.Dim_Customer c
    JOIN dwh.Fact_Orders f ON c.customer_id = f.customer_id
    GROUP BY customer_unique_id
)
SELECT
    CASE WHEN Lifetime_Orders = 1 THEN 'One-Time Buyer' ELSE 'Repeat Buyer' END AS Buyer_Type,
    COUNT(*) as Customer_Count,
    FORMAT(COUNT(*) * 1.0 / (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM CustomerFrequency), 'P') as Share_of_Base
FROM CustomerFrequency
GROUP BY CASE WHEN Lifetime_Orders = 1 THEN 'One-Time Buyer' ELSE 'Repeat Buyer' END;

Sellers and baskets: a few hundred sellers, single-product orders

Queries Q8, Q12 and Q13

What the numbers said

The top 10% of sellers, 310 of 3,095, take 67.6% of item revenue. The top 100 alone take 45.1%; the bottom half of sellers share 3.2%.

Big sellers are mostly well rated. Of the 20 with the most orders, only one averages below 3.5 stars (3.35 on 982 orders). Five sellers with more than 50 orders average under 3 stars, and together they hold 0.6% of revenue.

Almost nobody buys two different things: 3.3% of orders contain two different products and 0.8% span two categories. The most common pair, bed, bath & table with furniture & décor, appears together in 70 orders.

What it means

Losing a few top sellers would cost far more than any quality problem, so they need looking after. And with nearly every order a single product, basket size is a lever nobody has pulled yet.

Read the SQL: Q8 seller deciles 
-- Q8. Seller Concentration (Pareto Principle)
WITH SellerSales AS (
    SELECT seller_id, SUM(price) as Revenue
    FROM dwh.Fact_Order_Items
    GROUP BY seller_id
),
RankedSellers AS (
    SELECT Revenue, NTILE(10) OVER (ORDER BY Revenue DESC) as Decile
    FROM SellerSales
)
SELECT
    Decile,
    FORMAT(SUM(Revenue), 'C', 'en-US') as Total_Revenue,
    FORMAT(SUM(Revenue) * 1.0 / (SELECT SUM(Revenue) FROM SellerSales), 'P') as Share_of_Total
FROM RankedSellers
GROUP BY Decile
ORDER BY Decile;

Step 7: Turn the findings into actions

In
Four findings
Out
Eight actions, each with a number to watch

In the next three months

  1. 1
    Win the second order

    97% of customers buy once. Give first-time buyers whose order arrived on time a reason to come back, and hold out a control group.

    Watch: 90-day repeat rate against the control

  2. 2
    Promise dates carriers can keep

    Between 15% and 24% of orders arrive late in Alagoas, Maranhão, Piauí, Ceará and Sergipe, and reviews collapse past 20 days. Recalibrate the dates shown there and hold carriers to transit times.

    Watch: late rate by state and the share of 1–2 star reviews

  3. 3
    Chase the 1,101 stuck orders

    Each order sitting at “shipped” for 60+ days is a customer who paid and got nothing. Trace them with carriers, then refund or resend.

    Watch: orders stuck at “shipped” for more than 30 days

  4. 4
    Review the five lowest-rated large sellers

    Under 3 stars on 50+ orders, with 0.6% of revenue between them: cheap to coach or remove.

    Watch: their ratings and cancellations

In six to twelve months

  1. 5
    Look after the top 100 sellers

    They hold 45% of revenue. Account managers and terms worth staying for cost less than replacing them.

    Watch: top-100 revenue share and how many leave

  2. 6
    Test cross-selling on bedding and décor

    Only 3.3% of orders hold two different products. Pair bed, bath & table with furniture & décor in a “bought together” slot.

    Watch: attach rate and order value against a control

  3. 7
    Rethink shipping on small electronics

    On 22% of electronics orders, shipping costs more than the item. Test a basket minimum for flat-rate shipping.

    Watch: electronics conversion and freight per order

  4. 8
    Find out who buys on weekdays

    Weekday daytime peaks could be office workers or businesses. Ask at checkout before building anything for business buyers.

    Watch: share of orders placed by businesses

Not yet: a fulfilment hub in the North. Transit to Roraima averages 25.6 days, but Roraima, Amapá and Amazonas together had 254 delivered orders in two years, too few to pay for a warehouse today.

What carries over to an e‑commerce analyst role

  • Fix the grain before the join

    Items were summed to one row per order before joining, so a three-item order never counts three times.

  • Model once, ask many times

    One star schema and one view answered all fourteen questions with the same definitions.

  • Check the numbers against the source

    Rebuilding the queries outside SQL caught two traps: integer averages and percentages sorted as text.

  • End each finding with a test

    Every action above names the number that will show whether it worked.
